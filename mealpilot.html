01 — The Problem

Built for the wrong kitchen

Most meal planning apps are built for Western diets, Western budgets, and Western kitchens. They assume you have access to quinoa, a food processor, and the luxury of not worrying about how much you'll spend at the market this week.

For the average Nigerian, these apps are useless. And yet the problems they solve — what to cook, how to eat healthier, how to feed a child a balanced meal on a limited budget — are just as real, if not more urgent.

The Proof

A Nigerian commenter on LinkedIn said they didn't realise Nigeria had up to 50+ meals. That single comment — from someone inside the market — confirmed the gap MealPilot was built to close.

The specific gaps I identified through research:

🥗 The health myth People genuinely believe jollof rice and egusi can't fit into a diet plan. Nigerian food has been written off as "too carb-heavy" to be healthy.
💸 Budget paralysis Staring at ₦3,000 and not knowing what meal is actually possible with that amount — for someone eating to survive, not to plan.
🎒 Lunchbox stress Parents defaulting to whatever is quick, with no framework for nutrition on a limited budget. The goal is to feed the child — nutrition feels like a luxury.
💪 The gym gap Fitness-conscious Nigerians forced to choose between eating local and hitting their macros. No app bridged the two.
02 — The User

Meet Bola

To keep the design focused, I built MealPilot around one anchor user — someone who sits in the middle of the Nigerian income spectrum. Not the most vulnerable, not the most comfortable, but deeply real.

Bola, 34 · Lagos
Primary Persona

She recently quit her 9-to-5 to reskill for the modern job market. She's watching her budget closely, wants to eat well and stay healthy, but doesn't have the bandwidth to figure out what to cook every day. She's not looking for a Western diet app. She wants something that understands her kitchen, her wallet, and her taste.

Bola isn't just one person. She represents four distinct user needs that MealPilot is built to serve — and the product earns its "for everyone" positioning because of how it's structured, not through vague generality.

03 — Research

How I found the gap

MealPilot wasn't built from assumption. The insight came from four overlapping sources that gave me both emotional truth and pattern recognition.

Personal Experience

I lived this problem. The daily friction of Nigerian meal planning — what to cook, what it costs, whether it fits any health goal — is something I know firsthand.

Friends & Family

Conversations revealed patterns across income levels: the budget anxiety, the diet guilt, the lunchbox default, the market maths done in your head every morning.

Classroom Observation

Years of teaching showed me how parents approached feeding children on limited budgets. Nutrition was often the first casualty when money was tight.

Social Media Research

Comment sections, Twitter/X threads, and Facebook groups surfaced recurring pain points that crossed income levels — especially around healthy eating and Nigerian food.

What became clear: this wasn't a niche problem. It was a universal Nigerian experience with no product built specifically for it.

04 — The Solution

One app, four user realities

MealPilot is a Nigerian meal planning web app with four core sections — each targeting a specific user need, but all accessible from the same product. The goal: a place where the struggling and the comfortable both find something made for them.

01

Recipe Library — 50+ Nigerian Meals

A proper browsable library, not just a list of dishes. Ingredients, methods, and nutritional framing — challenging the myth that Nigerian food can't be varied or healthy.

02

Healthy & Diet / Gym Mode

Meal plans and recipe filters built around Nigerian ingredients — for users actively trying to lose weight, manage health conditions, or support a fitness routine. No substituting ogbono with kale.

03

Kids Lunchbox

A dedicated section for parents. Simple, nutritious, budget-appropriate meal ideas for school lunchboxes — designed for the average Nigerian parent feeding a child, not curating an Instagram post.

04

Budget Tool & Spend Tracker

The feature that makes MealPilot genuinely unique. Users input what they have — financially or as ingredients at home — and get meal suggestions that work within that constraint. "What can I make with ₦2,500 and the tomatoes in my kitchen?" MealPilot answers that.

05 — Design Decisions

The choices that shaped the product

Freemium per-card lock model. Core browsing is free; premium content is unlocked per card, not as a full subscription. Lower barrier to entry for budget-conscious users — the people who need the budget tool most aren't the ones who can afford a monthly subscription easily.

"~₦X,XXX to make" cost wording. Honest, approximate pricing on every recipe. Nobody is surprised at the market. The tilde matters — it signals realism, not false precision.

Dark mode with custom logo. Designed to feel modern and credible — not like a government nutrition pamphlet. Nigerian food deserved a product that looked as good as the food itself.

Supabase auth + row-level security. Secure, scalable login that supports the freemium unlock model without overengineering. Premium access is enforced at the database level.

Single web app, no install required. Works on any phone browser. No Play Store friction for users with limited device storage — a real constraint for many Nigerian users.

06 — Where It Stands

Live and validated

50+ Nigerian recipes in the library
4 Distinct user categories served
Live Deployed & accessible now

MealPilot is live and actively being expanded. The LinkedIn response at launch — including from Nigerians who were surprised by the breadth of the recipe library — validated the core insight: there was a real gap, and users felt it immediately when shown something actually built for them.

The Signal

When a Nigerian says "I didn't realise we had this many meals" — that's not embarrassing. That's product-market fit evidence. The gap wasn't just in the app; it was in how Nigerian food had been represented digitally.

07 — Reflection

What I learned

The most important design insight from MealPilot: solving for the average user doesn't mean building a lesser product. It means building a more honest one.

When you design for Bola — the person watching her budget, trying to eat well, feeding her child, figuring out her next move — you end up with a product that the comfortable user also finds useful. The reverse is rarely true.

MealPilot also showed me that AI-augmented building — using AI to accelerate ideation, content generation, and UI decisions while keeping human judgment central — produces faster, more considered results than either approach alone. The thinking still has to be yours. The tools just remove the friction between having an idea and seeing it work.
